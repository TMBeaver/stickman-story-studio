# Story bible

## Tone and theme

An original fictional fable. Start with warm amber against a rainy blue street; progress through wonder, regret, indignation and danger; end with simple morning warmth. The shop is unsettling, but the film keeps the accepted Cozy Storytelling drawing language rather than turning photorealistic or graphically violent.

The theme is demonstrated through choices: a father sacrifices a memory for his son; Milo initially gives up a memory for money, then risks losing it to help others; ultimately he chooses the living person beside him. The closing lemonade accident echoes the opening afternoon without pretending the lost memory has returned.

## Magic rules — fixed throughout

1. A sale removes **one specific episodic memory**. It does not erase a person's identity, general knowledge, all memories of a loved one or every emotion associated with them. Milo still knows his father, knows he died and recognizes the drawings. He cannot re-experience the particular afternoon he sold.
2. Extraction puts that moment into one sealed glass jar. The sale does not automatically destroy photographs, documents, drawings or other people's memories.
3. A viewing lens temporarily plays a stored memory, then returns its light to the same jar. The buyer sees it as a projection; the original owner has not regained it.
4. Breaking the wax seal releases an unburned memory back to its **original living owner**. The jar's glass body need not be shattered. A memory belonging to someone deceased can still be viewed in the device; it cannot resurrect them or transfer their life into someone else.
5. Burning the stored contents permanently destroys the memory. Breaking a seal before burning and burning a still-sealed jar have different outcomes. Establish this explicitly in part five before it matters in part nine.
6. Midnight is the shipment departure deadline, not an unexplained magical cutoff. It creates urgency; it does not introduce a new power or rule.
7. No secret backup, second copy, last-minute resurrection or refund restores the two burned hero jars. Lena retains her own perspective on that afternoon but cannot give Milo his first-person experience back by describing it.
8. The keeper's broader emotional distance results from **many separate sales over years**, not one sale erasing his daughter. Opening one jar starts his change; the surviving cart later returns the other preserved memories.

The birth-era flashbacks in part six dramatize the invoice and transaction described by the narrator. They are not Milo suddenly recovering his father's point of view or remembering his own birth.

## Drawing grammar

Minimalist 2D figures with white-filled circular heads, two small dark oval eyes, simple dark eyebrows and compact mouths. Slim closed white-filled torsos; short visible connecting necks; exactly two dark single-line arms and two dark single-line legs, small rounded dark hands and short dark feet. Medium consistent contours. Expressions change facial marks, head tilt, shoulders, hand position and weight. No chibi proportions, photoreal faces, detached floating heads, extra limbs or rhythmic speaking mouths.

The approved cafe look is the reference for environment richness and acting. This new cast uses restrained accessories for identification. Each appearing role's **complete anchor must be repeated** in every independent production prompt. A cast reference is not automatically the opening frame of a shot.

Memory-age changes are explicitly planned: infant Milo in part six; teenage Milo and younger Lena in the summer afternoon; younger father and keeper in the birth-era scenes. Preserve recognizability without shrinking adult clothing onto an infant. Restrict each generation to the cast actually needed.

The older woman in parts four/five, short town-memory recipients in part ten, and two officers/firefighter silhouettes are planned minor roles. Do not spawn random crowds or multiple copies of the main cast. Keep background roles sparse and subordinate.

Names, prices, invoices, jar titles, clock numerals and warning text are **post-production overlays**. Generate clean props without garbled lettering, then composite only the information essential to the spoken story.

## Shop geography

Maintain a simple, repeatable layout: street entrance -> counter -> archive aisles -> rear furnace workroom. A narrow internal service passage runs from the archive to a courtyard hatch. The courtyard gate opens onto the street. The rear workroom has a separate outer loading door.

The collector locks the **outer loading door** at the end of part six, then goes outside to arrange the shipment. He does not magically seal every interior route. The siblings find the service passage in part seven. The keeper enters that passage from the front archive, then unlocks the courtyard gate. In part eight the collector unlocks his own loading door to re-enter and pursue them. The falling shelving damages the furnace chute/control area and starts the fire.

Map the set before generation. Reuse references for returns and eyeline directions; never rely on prompt wording alone to preserve its geometry.

## Audio and captions

One stable English offscreen storyteller for all ten parts. Quoted remarks are performed by that same narrator without casting new speakers. No lip-syncing characters. Keep the voice close/dry even in memory or furnace scenes. A gentle three-note piano motif can connect warmth and loss; use quieter ambience and silence around the part-nine decision. Do not change narrator identity to signal emotion.

Captions are added locally from actual speech timings. Use natural short phrases and clear punctuation, with at most two readable lines. Do not put the next episode's reveal on screen early. Burning jar images and the final narration must remain synchronized.
