## Part 01 — The Price of Happiness

**Master target: 00:00–01:00 · 141 spoken words**

The first time Milo sold a memory, he asked if it would hurt. The shopkeeper said no. That was the first lie.

His rent was overdue. His drawing commissions had stopped. In his pocket, he had three coins and a key that might soon fit nothing.

The shop bought memories. A school prize paid almost nothing. A first kiss paid more. His happiest afternoon would cover the entire rent.

Milo chose a summer afternoon with his father. Two lemonades. A crooked drawing. His dad laughing so hard he could barely hold the pencil.

A warm light slipped into the jar. Milo took the money. He remembered his father's face, but that afternoon had become an empty space.

That evening, his sister found the receipt. Her smile vanished. Of all the afternoons, she whispered, you sold the last one we had together.

## Part 02 — No Refunds

**Master target: 01:00–02:00 · 139 spoken words**

Milo had sold his last afternoon with Dad. The next morning, their father had gone into hospital. He had never come home.

He knew those facts. He could repeat them. But he could no longer remember the last time his father had made him laugh.

He ran back with the money. The keeper did not touch it. Sold memories belonged to the auction house until a collector bought them.

At midnight, the jars would leave town. The sales were legal. The buyers were wealthy. And Milo could not afford his own happiness anymore.

Then he saw his jar on a packing cart. Beside it sat an older one, cloudy with dust, carrying his father's name.

Milo reached for the old receipt beneath it. The memory was called My Son's First Breath. His father had sold it twenty years ago.

## Part 03 — The Second Customer

**Master target: 02:00–03:00 · 140 spoken words**

Their father had sold the moment Milo was born. Lena checked the date twice. It was not a mistake. Neither was the payment.

The amount was enormous. Milo remembered a gentle man who patched his own coat. What could possibly have cost him that much money?

The keeper took the receipt away. Some people sell because they want more, he said. Some sell because someone else needs to live.

Before Milo could answer, a polished black car arrived. A buyer stepped out. The keeper straightened his coat and stopped looking at them.

Lena pulled her brother behind the shelves. Through a narrow doorway, they watched the buyer choose a memory and lower a glass viewing lens.

A stranger's childhood flickered across the wall. The buyer laughed at a father's joke. Milo recognized the crooked drawing. Someone was watching his afternoon.

## Part 04 — Someone Else's Happiness

**Master target: 03:00–04:00 · 140 spoken words**

A stranger was enjoying Milo's last afternoon with Dad. For the first time that night, being broke did not feel like the worst part.

The buyer called it charming. He asked whether the father had more memories available. The keeper said nothing. Milo nearly stepped out of hiding.

Then the lens shut. The afternoon returned to its jar. Milo watched the keeper remove it and lock it inside a padded case.

Lena had seen the key. She had also seen the delivery door. They could steal two jars, slip outside, and disappear before midnight.

They waited for the keeper to follow the buyer. Then Lena lifted the key while Milo opened the case with both hands shaking.

His sleeve caught a tiny jar on the shelf. It fell. The seal cracked. Somewhere outside, a woman began to cry with relief.

## Part 05 — The Broken Seal

**Master target: 04:00–05:00 · 142 spoken words**

The dropped jar had given its memory back. Outside, the woman pressed a hand to her mouth, remembering something she had thought was gone.

Milo picked up the glass. It was empty. Breaking a seal released the memory to its original owner. Keeping it sealed kept it trapped.

They could free their two jars right there. Instead, Lena turned toward the shelves. Hundreds of other people's happiest days waited in the dark.

A school concert. A wedding morning. A quiet goodbye. The little tags made each jar sound small. Together, they looked like an entire town.

Then they found the disposal chute. Broken glass went to a furnace. Its warning was simple: a burned memory could never be returned.

Beside the furnace stood their father's auction file. Inside was a hospital invoice. The patient was not their father. The patient was Milo.

## Part 06 — What Dad Paid

**Master target: 05:00–06:00 · 142 spoken words**

Milo had been sick as a baby. He knew the family story: a long hospital stay, a successful operation, a father who never complained.

What nobody had told him was how the bill got paid. His father had walked into this same shop with nothing left to sell.

Except one thing. The first time his baby had gripped his finger. A tiny hand. A whole new life. His happiest memory.

He traded that moment for the operation. Then he went home and learned to love his son without remembering the moment he first had.

Milo looked at his own sealed jar. Dad had lost a beginning to give him a future. Milo had sold their ending for rent.

He reached for the seal. Footsteps stopped behind them. The buyer was watching. He said the jars were his now, and locked the door.

## Part 07 — Two Jars or Everyone

**Master target: 06:00–07:00 · 143 spoken words**

The buyer locked them inside with the memories. He had seen the cracked jar. Now he wanted his shipment gone before they broke another.

Lena found a narrow service passage behind the shelves. At its end was the delivery hatch, wide enough for one person at a time.

They had a way out. Two jars. A clear street. Milo could go home with everything he had come to save that night.

But on the shelf beside him was a jar tagged with a child's drawing. A little girl holding the hand of an older man.

He put his jars in his satchel. Then he pulled an empty delivery cart toward the shelves. Lena watched him, sighed, and started loading.

A hand caught the cart before it rolled. The keeper stood in the passage. He looked at the girl's drawing and whispered, My daughter.

## Part 08 — The Keeper's Daughter

**Master target: 07:00–08:00 · 144 spoken words**

The keeper recognized the drawing, but not the afternoon inside the jar. He had sold it years ago, when keeping the shop seemed worth anything.

Over time, he had sold more. Her first song. Her ridiculous jokes. The sound of her coming home. He still knew her name.

But knowing a name, he said, was not the same as remembering a life. He had spent years protecting shelves he could not bear to empty.

He took the pink ribbon off the shelf. For a moment, Milo thought he would hide it. Instead, the keeper broke the seal.

The old man's face crumpled into a smile. Then he unlocked the courtyard gate. Take the cart, he said. Take every jar you can.

Behind them, the loading door slammed open. The buyer saw the escaping cart, grabbed a shelf, and tipped it toward the furnace controls.

## Part 09 — The Jar He Left Behind

**Master target: 08:00–09:00 · 141 spoken words**

The falling shelf tore open the furnace chute. Hot glass spilled across the floor. A curtain caught fire. The passage filled with smoke.

Outside, the keeper dragged the memory cart clear. Inside, Lena's coat caught beneath the fallen frame. Milo heard her call his name.

He pulled at the shelf. It would not move. He dropped his satchel, planted both feet, and lifted while Lena pulled herself free.

They stumbled into the rain. Only then did Milo see his bag inside, beside the fire. His last afternoon with Dad was still in it.

He took one step toward the doorway. Lena reached for him. For a second, he held the hand that was here, and wanted what wasn't.

Milo stayed. The ceiling gave way. Behind the falling sparks, two small lights went dark. His father's beginning. Their last afternoon. Both gone.

## Part 10 — What Was Still Here

**Master target: 09:00–10:00 · 142 spoken words**

Milo saved his sister. He could not save the jars. By morning, the shop was ash, and the cart stood untouched across the street.

They broke the seals one at a time. Warm lights rose above the rooftops. Across town, people stopped in doorways and remembered their own lives.

The keeper's daughter arrived before noon. He remembered her terrible jokes now. She told another. He laughed before she reached the ending.

The buyer demanded his property. Lena told the officers who had locked them inside. They took him aside. His money couldn't answer their questions.

That evening, Milo opened his sketchbook beside Lena. He tried to draw their father laughing. The face came easily. The afternoon did not.

Then Lena knocked over her lemonade. The drawing was ruined. They looked at each other, and laughed. This time, Milo wasn't missing the moment.
